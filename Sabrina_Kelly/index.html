01 — Programme Overview

16 weeks. Three levers. One outcome.

20 May – 9 September 2026 · Target: mid-September wedding

Your plan is built around three evidence-based levers that — in combination — produce body recomposition in trained and untrained women: progressive resistance training, a moderate energy deficit, and high protein intake. Cardio supports the deficit; it does not drive it. Sleep, stress and consistency drive the outcome more than the programme itself.

Why 10% per phase? Progressive overload is the central driver of muscle growth — the body adapts to whatever load you give it. Increasing volume gradually (by sets, weight, or reps) keeps the stimulus ahead of adaptation without overwhelming recovery. Jumping volume too fast inflates fatigue without inflating gains; raising it too slowly stalls progress.
Why 10 – 12 reps in Phase 1? This rep range hits the hypertrophy sweet spot for someone newer to lifting. It builds technique, conditions tendons and connective tissue, and creates muscle with less injury risk than heavy low-rep work. Once the patterns are grooved, Phase 2 drops the rep range to 8 – 12 with heavier loads.

Weeks 1 – 4: build the patterns

Lighter loads (50 – 65% of your eventual working weights), higher reps (10 – 15), and disciplined core work. Your two PT sessions land on the heaviest training days.

01 · Compound
Goblet Squat
3 × 10DB held at chest
Build the squat pattern before loading the bar. Focus on depth — hip crease below knee — and a tall brace. Drive through mid-foot, not toes.
02 · Compound
Deficit Single Leg Deadlift (Kettlebell)
3 × 8 each legLight KBSmall step or plate
Stand on a 2–5 cm step or plate. Hold the KB in the hand opposite the working leg. Hinge at the hip — push the hips back, keep a flat back, soft knee on the standing leg. The deficit lets the KB travel lower, giving your glute and hamstring a deeper stretch than a standard RDL. Pause when you feel a strong pull in the hamstring; don't round the lower back to go further. Drive through the heel to stand tall. The balance demand here is high — go slow, go light to start.
03 · Compound
Single-Arm Dumbbell Row
3 × 10 each
Drive elbow back to the hip, not up to the shoulder. Squeeze the shoulder blade at the top. Keep spine neutral — no twisting.
04 · Accessory
Glute Bridge
3 × 12Bodyweight or single DB
Push the floor away with heels. Tuck the pelvis at the top — do not arch lower back. Squeeze for one full second at peak.
05 · Core (anti-extension)
Dead Bug
3 × 8 each side
Lower back glued to the floor — non-negotiable. Opposite arm and leg lower slowly; return under control. If your back arches, reduce range.
06 · Core (anti-lateral-flexion)
Side Plank
3 × 20 – 30 sec each
Start on knees if needed — build to full plank. Hip lifted high, body in a straight line from head to base. Breathe normally.
07 · Accessory · Posterior Chain
Back Extension (45° Hyperextension Bench)
3 × 12Bodyweight or light plateFull ROM
Go lower than the pad angle suggests. Let your torso descend well past 45° — aim for near-vertical — until you feel a deep stretch in the glutes and hamstrings. Training the muscle in this lengthened position drives more hypertrophy than stopping halfway. Control the descent over 3 seconds.

At the top, go slightly past neutral and squeeze the glutes hard. This is hip hyperextension — not spinal hyperextension. The movement comes entirely from the hips: think of pushing your hips forward and tucking your pelvis. The glutes are powerful hip extensors, and those final degrees of extension are exactly where they fire hardest. Hold the squeeze for 1–2 seconds before lowering. You should feel it entirely in your glutes, not your lower back.

TRX Ab Crunch
3 × 10 – 12Feet in TRX handles
Start in a plank — hands under shoulders, body in a straight line, feet suspended in the TRX handles. Drive both knees toward your chest in a controlled crunch, flexing at the hip and spine. Pause briefly at the top, then extend slowly back to plank. Keep the hips level throughout — do not let them pike up or sag down. The slower the return, the more the abs work.

03 · Accessory · Glute Medius
Cable Hip Abduction
3 × 12 each legCable at ankle height
Attach the ankle cuff to the low cable pulley. Stand side-on to the machine, slight forward lean at the hip for balance. Keeping the leg straight, sweep it away from the body to about 45° — no hip hiking or torso leaning. Control the return slowly; resist the cable pulling the leg back in. This targets the glute medius, which shapes the side of the glute and stabilises the pelvis when you walk, run, and squat.
04 · Accessory · Rear Delt & Posture
Seated Face Pull (Cable)
3 × 12Seated on bench or floorRope attachment
Sit facing the cable stack with the pulley set at or just above head height. Pull the rope toward your face — elbows drive high and wide, hands finishing beside your ears. Sitting eliminates lower-body momentum, so your rear delts and upper back do all the work. Squeeze the shoulder blades together at the end of each rep and hold for one second. Your back and posture insurance policy.

2 × 12

06 · Accessory
Tricep Cable Pushdown
2 × 12
Elbows locked at sides — they don't move. Press the rope down and slightly apart at the bottom. Squeeze for one second.
Progression rule for Phase 1. When you can complete all prescribed reps with two reps still in reserve — meaning you could have done two more if pushed — add 1 – 2 kg to the working weight in the following session. Strength gains in Phase 1 are mostly neural; your brain learns to recruit muscle before the muscle itself grows. Both happen in Phase 2.
03 — Nutrition

The numbers, and why they're set there.

Calibrated to your InBody scan · 54.6 kg · BMR 1,208 kcal

Nutrition is the hinge. Train hard and eat carelessly and you'll plateau in week six. The numbers below are tight but not punishing — small enough to be sustainable for sixteen weeks, large enough to make weekly progress visible.

How the numbers are calculated
From BMR to daily target

BMR (basal metabolic rate) is the energy your body burns at complete rest — the price of being alive. Your InBody scan put yours at 1,208 kcal, cross-checked against the Mifflin-St Jeor equation (1,172 kcal) — consistent.

TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is BMR multiplied by an activity factor. With four resistance sessions and two to three walks each week, that factor is ~1.55 — putting your training-phase TDEE at 1,872 kcal/day.

A 400 kcal/day deficit puts your daily intake at ~1,470 kcal — producing weight loss of approximately 0.36 kg per week, or 0.67% of your body weight. This sits inside the 0.5 – 0.7% range that minimises lean-mass loss while making meaningful progress.

Why this size of deficit
Why not bigger and faster

Larger deficits (500 – 700 kcal) accelerate fat loss — but increase muscle loss risk. For petite women whose absolute calorie floor is lower to begin with, the relative cost of a 700 kcal deficit is far higher than for a taller woman with the same number on the scale.

One refeed day per week at maintenance (~1,880 kcal) restores muscle glycogen, supports leptin (the hormone signalling adequacy to the brain), and meaningfully improves long-term adherence. The weekly average remains ~1,530 kcal — still in a deficit, but psychologically and hormonally easier.

Place the refeed on your highest-volume training day, usually Monday or Friday.

Fixing the meal-skipping pattern

01
Three meals plus one shake or yoghurt snack. Don't try for six small meals — too high a friction cost when your schedule already moves quickly.
02
Fixed eating windows. 08:00, 13:00, 16:00, 19:30 — calendar them like meetings. Skipping a meeting is harder than skipping a meal.
03
Sunday food prep. Cook a protein (chicken or salmon), a carb (rice or sweet potato), and roasted vegetables in bulk. Combine into different meals through the week.
04
Track for four weeks. MyFitnessPal, Cronometer, or Macrofactor — Macrofactor adapts targets weekly and is the most evidence-aligned. Stop tracking once 110 g protein and 1,500 kcal feel intuitive.
